# Allergy as a Window into Self-Organization: Organizing Around Conflict Resolution

## The Starting Point

There is no shortage of people who want to work on conflict — between communities, institutions, nations. What is harder is organizing that work effectively. Efforts fragment, coalitions dissolve, organizations lose coherence, initiatives that begin with shared purpose end in internal dispute. The work of peace is itself subject to the same dynamics it is trying to address.

This suggests a prior question: before asking how to resolve conflict, ask **how do groups of people organize themselves coherently around a shared purpose, especially under conditions of stress and difference?**

That is a question about self-organization. And biology has been answering versions of it for hundreds of millions of years.

---

## What Self-Organization Actually Means

A self-organizing system produces and maintains its own coherence without a central controller. It has a boundary — some distinction between what is inside and what is outside — and that boundary is not given in advance. It is continuously produced by the system's own activity.

This is true of cells, organisms, ecosystems. It is also true of communities, movements, institutions, and nations. None of these have a fixed, pre-given identity. They are continuously producing themselves through the interactions of their parts, at their boundaries, over time.

Understanding how that process works — what makes it stable, what makes it fail, what allows it to remain open and adaptive rather than rigid or dissolved — is directly relevant to anyone trying to build organizations capable of sustained, effective work on conflict.

---

## Why Biology, and Why Allergy Specifically

Biological systems are the most thoroughly studied examples of self-organization we have. They offer something social theory currently lacks: mechanistic, physical accounts of how coherent identity is produced and maintained over time, under pressure, at scale.

But biological self-organization, when it works, is largely invisible. The organism maintains itself, processes its environment, adapts — and we cannot easily see the underlying mechanism.

**Allergy is the place where the mechanism becomes visible.**

Allergy is not best understood as a defense system malfunctioning. It is the surface where the distinction between self and other is genuinely not well-defined — where the organism is doing real, visible computational work to determine what belongs to it and what does not. Studying allergy is studying the boundary-definition process at the moment it is working hardest and showing its structure most clearly.

---

## Why Start with Spiders

Humans are too complicated to start with. Our self-organization is layered with culture, language, history, and institutional structure in ways that make it difficult to isolate what is fundamental.

Simpler organisms offer cleaner signal. **Spiders** are a useful entry point:

- They self-organize without central coordination, producing complex, stable structures (webs, territories, physiological coherence) from local interactions
- Silk is produced *by* the spider but exists *outside* it — and yet the spider does not react to its own silk. This is a boundary decision being made continuously, at the edge of self, without a controller
- Their boundary-definition processes are legible at multiple scales simultaneously

The goal is not to stay with spiders. It is to understand the mechanism where it is tractable, then ask carefully what generalizes — to more complex organisms, and eventually to human communities and organizations.

---

## What Allergy Reveals About Boundary Definition

Four things that allergy makes visible, each with direct relevance to how human organizations function:

**Boundaries have memory.** Sensitization shows that prior exposure shapes future classification. The system learns — but learning from past threat can produce reactions that are miscalibrated to present reality. Organizations and communities carry the same kind of memory. Understanding how boundary-memory forms and how it can be recalibrated is foundational to understanding why groups behave as they do.

**Boundaries are contextual.** The same input triggers reaction in one context and not another. The system is integrating current state with incoming signal, not just pattern-matching. This means there is no purely content-based account of why a boundary holds or fails — context and relational state always matter.

**The boundary is genuinely fuzzy at the interface.** There is no sharp line between self and other. There is a region — at the skin, the gut lining, the mucosa — where the system continuously samples, tolerates, and classifies. This is where the real work happens. Human organizations have equivalent regions: the edge of the group, the point of contact with other groups, the places where membership and belonging are actively negotiated. These interfaces are not problems. They are where adaptive capacity lives.

**Tolerance is active and costly.** The gut must tolerate enormous non-self load — microbiome, food, environment — or it destroys itself. That tolerance is not passive. It is maintained continuously, under metabolic cost, and it can fail under overload. Organizations that want to remain genuinely open to difference face the same dynamic. Tolerance capacity is real, finite, and needs to be actively protected. When it is overwhelmed, the system attacks what it needs.

## Toward a Substrate-Independent Framework

The goal is a description of self-organization that applies across biological and human systems — not as metaphor, but as the same underlying physics operating at different scales and substrates.

Some existing frameworks point toward this:

**Phase boundaries (thermodynamics)** — The boundary between phases is not a line but a region with its own distinct properties. The interface behaves differently from the interior. Organizations that try to make their boundaries sharp and clear often lose the adaptive capacity that lives in the interface region.

**Dissipative structures (Prigogine)** — A self-organizing system maintains itself by being *open in a controlled way* — exchanging energy and matter with its environment, exporting entropy. The boundary is the site of organized exchange, not a wall. A system that seals itself to protect coherence destroys the openness it needs to survive.

**Scale-dependence** — What counts as "self" depends on the scale of observation. A person is self to themselves; a team is self to an organization; an organization is self to a movement. Many failures of coordination involve parties operating at different scales of self-definition simultaneously. Effective organizing requires clarity about which scale is operative at any moment.

**Information and model-updating** — The system maintains a model of itself and updates it at the boundary, where new signal arrives. Conflict within organizations often involves competing models of what the organization is — disagreements about self-definition that are experienced as disagreements about strategy or values.

## What This Means for Organizing Around Conflict Resolution

People and organizations working on conflict face a specific version of the self-organization problem: they must maintain coherent identity and purpose while remaining genuinely open to difference — including the difference and difficulty that comes with the work itself.

Understanding self-organization mechanisms suggests some reframes:

**The organization doing conflict work is itself subject to boundary-definition pressures.** It will develop its own sensitizations, its own tolerance limits, its own internal disputes about what belongs inside and what does not. These are not failures of commitment or personality. They are structural features of any self-organizing system under stress. Recognizing them as such changes how they are addressed.

**Coherence and openness are in genuine tension, and both are necessary.** A group that becomes too defined — too clear about what it is and who belongs — loses the adaptive capacity to work across difference. A group that loses definition dissolves. The productive place is the maintained tension between the two: enough coherence to act, enough openness to learn. Biological systems that do this well offer concrete models.

**Interface regions are where the work actually happens.** The most important activity in a conflict-resolution organization is not in the interior — the meetings, the frameworks, the internal culture. It is at the edges: the relationships with communities in conflict, the encounters with difference and difficulty, the moments where the organization's self-definition is actively tested. Designing those interfaces well matters more than designing the interior.

**Tolerance capacity needs to be managed, not assumed.** Organizations doing hard work in hard contexts will have their tolerance capacity stressed. Building in recovery time, managing the rate of difficult input, and recognizing when the system is overloaded are practical organizational design questions, not just wellness concerns.

**Scale clarity matters.** Conflicts about mission, strategy, or values within conflict-resolution organizations are often actually conflicts about scale — about which level of self-organization the group is trying to be coherent at. Making that explicit can dissolve disputes that otherwise seem intractable.

## The Deeper Ambition

If a rigorous, mechanistic account of self-organization can be developed — grounded in biology, formalized in physics, and tested across scales — it becomes a shared resource. Not a tool applied to communities in conflict from outside, but something that communities themselves, and the organizations trying to support them, can use to understand their own dynamics.

The aspiration is a science of self-organization that is:

- **Substrate-independent** — applicable to cells, organisms, communities, and institutions through the same underlying principles
- **Scale-aware** — able to move coherently between the individual, the group, the institution, and the nation
- **Practically useful** — offering not just description but orientation for people doing the actual work of building coherent organizations in difficult conditions

That science does not yet fully exist. Building it starts with spiders, with allergy, with the physics of interfaces and dissipative structures — and scales toward the questions that matter most.

How do groups of people organize themselves coherently around shared purpose, under stress, across difference? How do they maintain the openness they need without losing the coherence that makes action possible? How do they recognize when their boundary processes have gone wrong, and what do they do about it?

These are old questions. They may have answers that are closer to physics than we have assumed.

*Research direction: comparative immunology in simple organisms as a tractable model for self-organization and boundary-definition mechanisms. Grounded in the physics of interface phenomena and dissipative structures. Scaled toward practical understanding of how organizations and communities can better organize around conflict resolution.*
